Designing Tactile Communication

Exploring reproducible methods for translating visual art and information into tactile graphics.

How can art and information be shared through touch?

Maps, diagrams, charts, and works of art help people learn about the world around us; however, when information is shared only in visual format, it can create barriers for those who are Blind or have Low-Vision.

Tactile graphics offer additional ways to explore and understand information, incorporating raised lines, different textures, and other elements that can be felt with fingers and hands.

In museums, objects and artwork are often displayed in cases and behind frames that separate the viewer and create harsh glare. Tactile objects help bridge this gap, connecting audiences of varying abilities to visualize subject matter through touch.

What we’re doing

We are a small, interdisciplinary team of undergraduate students at Lehigh University exploring affordable, reproducible methods for creating tactile graphics. We test a range of materials and tools, from makerspace technologies like 3D printers and the swell machine to DIY techniques like felting and embroidery.

This website is where our findings live. Our goal is to make it an accessible resource for the Blind and Low-Vision community, educators, nonprofits, and community organizations, so that tactile graphics become more widely available and easier to create!

Blog & Research

Notes from the work

The Big Picture

A lot of art and information is graphics centered, and there are increasing gaps in art accessibility. The use of tactiles is not widespread, and resources are unequally distributed.

A Human-Centered View

Tactiles are not treated as a standard feature, and quality and implementation are not very high. Low quality tactiles can make a museum experience frustrating, and even bad. Our idea: make tactile information more accessible.

How is the system set up?

Museums have historically emphasized “look but don’t touch,” prioritizing preservation and visual contemplation over multisensory engagement. Blind visitors also have less autonomy compared to sighted visitors who can walk freely through galleries.

The Exhibition

Please Touch!

November 4, 2026
LUAG Lab, Lehigh University Art Galleries

Please Touch! is an exhibition-style representation of our student research project, exploring reproducible methods for translating visual art and information into tactile graphics.

The installation highlights our tactile translations of Head of Man by Leonard Baskin and Every Cotton Ball is a Moment of Pain by Gary Graves. Explore the exhibition and learn how tactile objects help bridge this gap, connecting audiences of varying abilities through touch.
